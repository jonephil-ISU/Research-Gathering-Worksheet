What This Worksheet Is For

Your notes: Section A, "Evidence turns a complaint into a case," in your Week 2 Interactive Reading PDF (the part that says "Worksheet notes"). Or find the same section in the Reading Only. You wrote your ask, your complaint, and your case. Keep them handy. Step 1 is where your ask and your problem go on the page.

This is where you build the case, not where you get handed one. Nothing in here gets generated for you. You do the thinking, you do the research, you fill in what you actually found.

There are two big jobs in this worksheet. First, you turn the situation from Week 1 into a Theoretical Framework, a named pattern that explains why this problem is real and ongoing, not a one-time fluke. Second, you go find real evidence in three rounds: proof this is a recognized problem, hard numbers comparing your options, and real voices from people who've actually used the equipment. The numbers are quantitative evidence and the voices are qualitative evidence, and a strong case uses both. Every source you use needs a real, working link. No made-up sources, no vendor pages pretending to be independent reviews. This week you find and save your sources. Week 3 is where you dig into them.

Work through this in order. Each screen explains why it matters before it asks you anything, so read the explanation, then answer for real. Short, vague answers lead to weak writing. Specific answers, with names, numbers, and real links, lead to a strong proposal. Write it like you are explaining it to your supervisor.

STEP 2

Build Your Theoretical Framework

Your notes: Sections C and D in your Week 2 Interactive Reading PDF. Section C, "Find the pattern yourself," is your plan for the pattern questions. Section D, "Name the concept," is your plan for naming the concept. Both are also in the Reading Only.

A theoretical framework is the named pattern behind your problem. Right now you have a list of individual complaints. Without a named pattern connecting them, a supervisor watching the budget can wave each one off as a bad day or a rare fluke. The framework turns isolated incidents into a single, provable, ongoing problem. Everything you gather later exists to prove this specific pattern is real. If you need a reminder of what a framework is and why it matters, reread the Quick Concept page in your Interactive Reading. Use full sentences and specific details, like numbers, amounts, and examples. A few words will not be enough for Week 3.

Part 1: Find the Pattern Yourself

Think about real changes in the work itself, like job length, volume, or materials, not the symptom you're seeing.

Point to a specific moment, like when people started noticing it or when it got flagged, not a vague sense that it got worse.

Part 2: Name the Concept

Now go find out what this pattern is actually called. This named pattern becomes the Theoretical Framework section of your research proposal. Search for the real academic or professional concept that matches what you just described, in a textbook, a course reading, a credible article, or a field you can name. You are naming a real concept, not inventing one.

Name the researcher, field, textbook, or course material it traces back to. If you found it online, paste the link. Pick a source your supervisor would trust. Make sure you can explain what the source says and why you trust who wrote it. Not sure where to start? Follow the 7-step "How to find your framework" roadmap in Section D, "Name the concept," of your Interactive Reading or Reading Only. Quick links: ISU Library: Find Articles | Google Scholar.

Name at least one real alternative explanation you considered, and say why your concept fits better.

Coach's Corner: If you can't name a real researcher, field, or source this traces back to, you haven't found the real concept yet, you've made one up. Keep looking.

STEP 4

Round Two (Quantitative Data)

Your notes: Section F, "Round Two: finding the numbers," in your Week 2 Interactive Reading PDF (the part that says "Worksheet notes"). Or find the same section in the Reading Only. Use it as your plan for this step. Section F has "Where to look" and "Rules for good sources."

Quantitative data means numbers: cost, specs, capacity, speed, warranty length, whatever is actually measurable about the equipment you're comparing. This round is strictly numbers and specs. You are finding sources that have the numbers, not pulling the numbers out yet. That happens in Week 3. Save opinions and reviews for Round Three. A video that shows the numbers, like a timed job or a spec walkthrough, can be a source too. You are comparing two different brands of the same type of product. Each one gets its own source this week. These become the two options you give your supervisor in Week 3. If your source is the manufacturer's own spec sheet, try to find an independent test or review of the same product too, and name it in "What else did you find." Fill in every box for every source. The title, who wrote it, and where you found it are what Week 3 works from.

STEP 5

Round Three (Qualitative Data)

Your notes: Section G, "Round Three: finding real voices," in your Week 2 Interactive Reading PDF (the part that says "Worksheet notes"). Or find the same section in the Reading Only. Use it as your plan for this step. Section G has "Where to look" and "Ask who is speaking."

Qualitative data means real experience: reviews, forum posts, interviews, videos from people in your trade, or accounts from people who have actually worked with this kind of equipment. This round needs independent voices, not marketing copy. A glowing product description on the seller's own site does not count as a review. You are finding the voices now. In Week 3 you will dig into what they said. For a video, save the link to the video, not the channel, and note the speaker's name and role. Fill in every box for every source.
